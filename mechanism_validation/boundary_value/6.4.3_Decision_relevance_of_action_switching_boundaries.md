### 6.4.3 Decision relevance of action-switching boundaries

Section 6.4.2 shows that the decision consequence of a preference ambiguity set depends on whether it crosses action-switching boundaries. This raises a further question: does crossing more or denser boundaries necessarily imply a larger decision consequence, and should every boundary-crossing ambiguity be clarified? In a human–machine decision system with costly interaction, a switching boundary is relevant to clarification only when the action improvement supported by resolving it is sufficient to offset the interaction cost.

We use the 65 unique switching boundaries identified in Section 6.4.1 as the units of analysis. Around each boundary, we construct a local ambiguity window of width 0.04 and record the number and spacing of the switching boundaries contained in that window. A boundary is classified as isolated when it is the only boundary in the window and as clustered otherwise. For the two adjacent actions, we calculate their normalized time and information-loss differences and the regret incurred when either action is applied on the opposite side of the boundary. We then solve the minimax-regret problem for every local window to optimality.

The calibrated interaction cost is (C^{\mathrm{int}}=0.0094). If the minimax regret of a boundary window satisfies

\[
R_j^{\mathrm{MMR}}
\leq
C^{\mathrm{int}},
\]

then even perfect clarification cannot produce an action improvement exceeding its cost, because

\[
V_j^{\max}
\leq
R_j^{\mathrm{MMR}}-C^{\mathrm{int}}
\leq0.
\]

These boundaries are therefore identified directly as cost-dominated. For the remaining boundaries, we evaluate all adjacent-action queries using the calibrated indifference threshold, response-noise scale, cumulative error budget, and interaction cost. Each feedback branch is reoptimized exactly, and the query with the largest expected net action improvement is retained.

Among the 65 switching boundaries, 50 are locally isolated and 15 belong to boundary clusters. Their mean minimax-regret values are 0.00809 and 0.00863, respectively, with strongly overlapping ranges. The descriptive rank correlation between the local boundary count and minimax regret is 0.033, while that between the nearest-boundary gap and minimax regret is −0.005. By contrast, the maximum cross-boundary regret has a rank correlation of 0.966 with minimax regret. Boundary density therefore provides little information about the consequence of the remaining ambiguity, whereas the loss associated with confusing the actions on the two sides of a boundary closely tracks the robust decision risk.

Of the 65 boundary windows, 41 (63.08%) are cost-dominated. The remaining 24 have minimax regret above the interaction cost and are evaluated through the complete feedback model. Their best expected action improvement ranges from 0.00060 to 0.00592, with a mean of 0.00145, which remains below \(C^{\mathrm{int}}\). Consequently, their best net query values range from −0.00880 to −0.00348. Under the calibrated 0.04 local ambiguity scale, none of the 65 switching boundaries supports a positive-net-value clarification query.

The individual boundaries further illustrate the separation between density, consequence, and clarification value. A clustered boundary in the \(9\text{-}3\text{-}RC\) instance with seed 20270110 has two local switching boundaries but a minimax regret of only 0.00091 and is directly cost-dominated. Another clustered boundary in the \(9\text{-}3\text{-}C\) instance with seed 20261117 has a minimax regret of 0.02120, yet its best expected action improvement is 0.00342 and its net query value is −0.00598. The largest minimax regret, 0.02775, occurs at an isolated boundary in the \(9\text{-}3\text{-}R\) instance with seed 20261213; its best expected improvement is 0.00592 and remains below the interaction cost.

Table X shows that boundary density does not alter the cost conclusion. The mean minimax regret differs only slightly between isolated and clustered boundaries, and neither group contains a positive-net-value query. At the calibrated ambiguity width of 0.04 and under the current human–machine interaction configuration, the potential benefit of additional preference information is insufficient to support another sequential clarification round. The system has therefore reached cost-aware decision sufficiency, although the remaining ambiguity may still span multiple action regions.

Preference uncertainty is not equivalent to decision uncertainty. Action-switching boundaries determine when unresolved preference ambiguity can alter the operational action, while cross-boundary regret determines whether the resulting action ambiguity has material consequences. In a human–machine system with costly clarification, however, the presence of action ambiguity does not by itself justify further preference elicitation. Decision sufficiency is reached when the remaining ambiguity cannot support an expected action improvement large enough to offset the cost of acquiring additional preference information.

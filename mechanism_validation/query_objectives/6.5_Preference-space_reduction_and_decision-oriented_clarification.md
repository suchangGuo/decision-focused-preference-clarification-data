## 6.5 Preference-space reduction and decision-oriented clarification

Reducing preference ambiguity is not equivalent to improving the operational decision. An interval may remain relatively wide while lying largely within an action-equivalence region, whereas a much narrower interval may retain ambiguity around a consequential action-switching boundary. Preference-space contraction is therefore an incomplete proxy for the decision value of clarification. To examine this distinction, we compare a width-oriented policy, which selects the query with the largest expected reduction in ambiguity-set width, with a decision-oriented policy, which selects the query with the largest expected improvement after response-contingent action reoptimization.

The comparison uses the 15 formal instances from Section 6.4.1. Both policies start from the same ambiguity set, \([0.30,0.70]\), and share the same candidate queries, feedback model, ambiguity-set update rule, and maximum budget of three queries. Candidate queries compare adjacent actions at the exact switching boundaries. Five hidden preference values, \(\lambda^*\in\{0.34,0.42,0.50,0.58,0.66\}\), are considered for each instance. The three possible responses to each query are enumerated exactly, so the reported results contain no Monte Carlo sampling error. Interaction cost is excluded in this experiment to isolate the effect of the query objective; cost-sensitive stopping is examined separately in Section 6.6.

The two query scores are positively related but not equivalent. Across the 65 initial candidate queries, their Spearman rank correlation is 0.857, while the selected initial query differs in 8 of the 15 instances. Within these eight instances, the width-oriented policy achieves an average expected width reduction of 0.19497, compared with 0.18009 under the decision-oriented policy. The decision-oriented policy instead achieves an average expected action improvement of 0.07600, compared with 0.06818 under the width-oriented policy. The width-oriented policy thus produces 8.26% more interval contraction, while the decision-oriented policy produces 11.47% more expected action improvement.

Table X shows the distinction at the decision level. After one query, the decision-oriented policy leaves an ambiguity interval that is 3.42% wider on average, but reduces expected minimax regret by 4.99% and expected realized regret by 22.53%. Its probability of selecting the oracle action is also 2.52 percentage points higher. The additional geometric contraction obtained by the width-oriented policy therefore does not translate into a better operational decision.

With repeated clarification, both policies recover additional decision-relevant information and their aggregate performance converges. After three query opportunities, the mean expected realized regrets are 0.00125 and 0.00128 for the width-oriented and decision-oriented policies, respectively. Their ordering, however, remains scenario dependent: in 14 of the 75 paired scenarios, the width-oriented policy produces a smaller final ambiguity set but a higher realized regret. Preference-width reduction therefore provides neither a sufficient representation of decision uncertainty nor a stable ordering of clarification outcomes.

The experiment identifies the distinction between learning preferences and improving decisions. In a discrete operational problem, clarification value is determined by how feedback changes the action supported by the remaining ambiguity and the regret associated with that action. Query selection should therefore be evaluated through response-contingent operational improvement, rather than through preference-space contraction alone.

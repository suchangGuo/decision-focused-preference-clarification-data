### 6.6.X Interaction-cost boundary analysis

Interaction cost defines the marginal value threshold for acquiring an additional preference response. We examine this boundary at \(C^{\mathrm{int}}\in\{0,0.0047,0.0094,0.0188\}\), while retaining the 15 formal instances, five hidden-preference positions, initial ambiguity set, feedback model, and five-round limit used in the preceding experiment. The feedback branches are enumerated exactly. The four levels include a zero-cost reference, one-half of the calibrated value, the calibrated value, and twice that value.

**Figure X. Terminal-state composition under different interaction-cost levels.** Each bar reports the terminal probability mass aggregated over the formal instances and hidden-preference positions.

Table X and Figure X show that interaction cost changes which stopping boundary becomes binding. With zero interaction cost, 18.79% of the terminal probability mass reaches the resource limit, whereas only 8.87% terminates at the value boundary. Once interaction cost becomes positive, resource-constrained termination disappears and cost-aware termination rises to 61.69% at \(C^{\mathrm{int}}=0.0047\), 78.55% at 0.0094, and 81.19% at 0.0188. The declining structural share indicates that a growing proportion of trajectories terminates at the value boundary before reaching a later infeasible feedback update. Action-stable termination appears at the two lower cost levels but accounts for no more than 0.22% of the terminal probability mass. Action invariance therefore cannot serve as the sole stopping principle in the human-in-the-loop system. Under zero or very low interaction cost, clarification proceeds sufficiently far for structural feasibility and resource limits to determine most terminal states, making the design of admissible feedback updates and structural boundaries central. As interaction cost increases, the cost-aware boundary becomes dominant, shifting attention toward interaction-cost calibration and the assessment of marginal decision value. The stopping architecture must therefore represent both structural and economic boundaries, whose relative importance changes with the interaction-cost regime.

Most of the adjustment occurs between zero cost and the calibrated cost range, with smaller changes at higher cost levels. This joint pattern reveals a cost-induced preference-resolution effect: higher collaboration costs reduce the operational value of distinguishing fine-grained preference differences and thereby lower the effective resolution pursued by the system. Preference clarification is therefore governed by the resolution required for cost-effective action selection, rather than by maximal preference identification.

Table X and Figure X further show that earlier stopping carries an operational consequence. Mean MMR increases from 0.00702 to 0.02511 across the four cost levels, while mean realized regret increases from 0.00084 to 0.00439. The common direction of these two measures shows that the additional ambiguity retained under higher interaction cost is not merely geometric. It enlarges both the robust exposure represented by MMR and the loss realized under the hidden preference. The increase nevertheless flattens toward the highest cost level, consistent with the saturation observed in the terminal-state composition and query depth.

Taken together, the results identify interaction cost as an operational design lever for preference resolution in human–machine systems. Decision precision can be improved not only through more informative queries or more accurate preference models, but also by reducing the response burden, interaction delay, and opportunity cost of each clarification. Lower clarification cost shifts the stopping boundary and makes finer preference differences worth resolving, thereby narrowing residual ambiguity and reducing decision risk. Preference-learning design and collaboration-system design are therefore tightly coupled: improvements to the interaction process can raise the effective resolution of managerial preferences even without changing the underlying elicitation model.
